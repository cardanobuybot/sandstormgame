## What the game is

Dune Clash is a build-and-raid strategy game, the same family as Clash of Clans. A player builds a base on a desert planet, collects oil and scrap, and sends soldiers, tanks and aircraft to attack other players' bases in battles that last three minutes. Other players attack theirs in the same way. A giant sand worm turns up in some battles and eats whatever is standing on the sand.

It is a game of planning: what to build first, how to lay out a base, which troops to send and where. Sessions are short. Much of the game is waiting for buildings to finish, which takes real time, from seconds to days.

## Content

- **Violence** is frequent and mild. Tiny cartoon soldiers shoot at buildings, buildings burn and collapse, units fall over when defeated. There is no blood or gore, and nothing is shown close up.
- **The worm** bursts out of the sand and bites. It is drawn as a cartoon monster. Very young children might find it startling.
- **Language:** no profanity in the game's text. The only words written by other players are their commander names (see below).
- **No** sexual content, drugs, alcohol, gambling or horror.

## Contact with other players

This is usually the question that matters most, so plainly:

- There is **no chat**: no text chat, no voice, no private messages.
- There are **no friend lists** and **no clans or groups**.
- There is **no way to send anything** to another player, or to receive anything from one.

What players do see of each other:

- A **commander name**, up to 16 characters, which each player chooses. It appears when someone raids a base, in the battle log and on the ladder.
- The **base** itself, when it is raided.

Names are checked against a filter when they are chosen. Next to every name there is a flag: tapping it hides that name for your child at once and reports it to us; a name reported by several players is reset to a neutral one. We also read reports sent by e-mail.

**Our advice:** help your child choose a commander name that is not their real name and gives away nothing personal.

One thing to explain to a younger child: their base *will* be attacked by other players while they are away, and they will lose some oil and scrap. That is the game, not bullying. Nothing they have built is ever destroyed for good, and nobody can single them out: opponents are picked by the game.

## Money

The game is free and has no adverts.

It sells one thing: **crystals**, in five packs from US $0.99 to US $49.99 (shown in your local currency). Crystals shorten waiting times. They do not unlock anything that cannot be earned by playing, and the game hands out a modest number for free.

- Purchases exist only in the **iPhone and Android apps** and always go through Apple or Google. The game never asks for card details.
- The **browser version sells nothing**.
- There are **no loot boxes** or other purchases with random contents. A pack always contains exactly the stated number of crystals.
- Purchases are final, apart from the refund rules of Apple and Google.

The honest caveat: timers are how games like this create the wish to pay. A child who is impatient to see an upgrade finish will see a **Finish now** button with a crystal price on it. If your child has access to a payment method, set a purchase control.

### How to switch purchases off

**On an iPhone or iPad:** open *Settings*, then *Screen Time*, then *Content & Privacy Restrictions*, then *iTunes & App Store Purchases*, and set *In-app Purchases* to *Don't Allow*. With Family Sharing you can instead turn on *Ask to Buy*, so that every purchase needs your approval.

**On Android:** in the Google Play app, open the settings and require authentication for every purchase. With Google's Family Link you can require your approval for purchases.

The exact wording of these menus changes between system versions.

More about what crystals cost and do is in the [crystals guide](/blog/dune-clash-crystals-guide/).

## Personal data

Dune Clash was built to need very little.

**It does not ask for or store** a real name, e-mail address, phone number, date of birth, location, contacts or photos. There is no sign-up: the game creates a guest account with a random identifier.

**It does store:** the commander name your child chooses, their base and battle records, and, if they use it, an ID number that links the base to Game Center or a Google account so it can be restored on a new phone. For purchases it keeps the transaction record that the store sends.

**It contains no advertising and no analytics or tracking software.** Data is kept on servers in the European Union and is not sold or shared for marketing.

**Deleting everything** takes two taps: menu ☰, then *Delete account*, and confirm. The full policy is short: [privacy policy](https://dune-of-clash.vercel.app/privacy.html).

The game is not directed at children under 13 and does not knowingly collect personal information from them.

## Screen time

Some things in the design work in a parent's favour, and one does not.

- **Sessions end naturally.** Once the builders are busy and the stores are full, there is little left to do for a while. A visit can be as short as five minutes.
- **The game sends no notifications.** It does not call your child back.
- **There are no daily task lists or streaks** to keep up. One small reward exists for the first won battle of the day.
- **But** timers pull players back to collect and to start the next upgrade. That is the hook of this whole genre, and it is worth a conversation about checking in versus being pulled in.

If you want hard limits, the Screen Time settings on iPhone and Digital Wellbeing or Family Link on Android can cap daily minutes for a single app.

## Fairness

Children notice cheating and hate it. In Dune Clash the result of every battle is worked out by the server, not by the player's phone, so results cannot be faked with a modified app. [How that works](/blog/fair-play-server-replays-anti-cheat/).

## What a child might get out of it

We will not call a raiding game educational. But it does reward things worth practising: planning several steps ahead, simple arithmetic with large numbers, patience, and learning from a loss by watching the replay to see what went wrong.

## Is it right for my child?

- **Under 9:** probably not. The reading level, the numbers and losing resources to other players are a lot for young children.
- **9 to 12:** the App Store rating allows it, but the game is not directed at children under 13, so this one is your call. If you say yes, switch purchases off first and help choose the commander name.
- **13 and up:** this is who we make it for.

If you would like to see it before deciding, the game runs in a web browser with nothing to install and nothing to buy: [play Dune Clash in your browser](/blog/play-dune-clash-in-browser/). Twenty minutes will show you everything described here.
