Build a base. Gather something. Make an army. Go and knock down somebody else's base. People have been playing that loop for more than thirty years, on machines that have almost nothing else in common. Here is how it got from a beige desktop computer to your pocket.

## 1989 to 1992: strategy stops taking turns

Early strategy games were board games on a screen: you moved, then the computer moved. The idea of everybody moving at once came slowly and from several directions.

One landmark is **Herzog Zwei**, made by the Japanese studio Technosoft for the Sega Mega Drive in 1989. You flew a transforming robot around a battlefield, bought units and carried them to the front while your opponent did the same. It is often named as the first real-time strategy game in the modern sense.

The game that turned the idea into a genre came three years later. **Dune II**, made by Westwood Studios and published by Virgin Games at the end of 1992, was set on the desert planet of Frank Herbert's novels. It put the pieces together in the order everybody has used since:

- a **base** you build piece by piece, where each building unlocks others;
- a **resource** you have to go out and harvest, and can lose;
- **factions** with different units;
- a **mouse**, pointing at things and telling them where to go.

The people who made it have said that Herzog Zwei was one of their influences. Westwood's own Brett Sperry is credited with coming up with the label "real-time strategy" to describe what they had built.

Dune II also had a danger that belonged to nobody: sandworms that swallowed harvesters and tanks crossing open sand. Thirty years on, a neutral monster under the dunes is still a good idea. More about that in [giant sand worms in fiction and games](/blog/sand-worms-in-games-and-fiction/).

## 1994 to 1998: the golden age

What followed is the part most people remember. Blizzard's **Warcraft: Orcs & Humans** (1994) moved the formula to a fantasy world. Westwood answered with **Command & Conquer** (1995). **StarCraft** (1998) became a sport.

These games were about one intense match: half an hour, both players present, a winner at the end. Then you started again from nothing. That turned out to be the one thing a later generation would change.

## 2002 to 2004: the base that never switches off

In the early 2000s a different kind of strategy game appeared in web browsers. **OGame** (2002) and **Travian** (2004), both from Germany, needed no installation and had only the simplest graphics. What they had was a world that kept running after you closed the tab.

Three ideas from that era are in every mobile strategy game today:

- **Persistence.** Your base is a place that exists for weeks or months, not a match that lasts half an hour.
- **Timers.** A building takes hours in real time. You give orders, leave, and come back.
- **Raiding people who are not there.** Other players attack your base while you are asleep and carry off your resources, so what you do before logging off matters.

Check in for ten minutes, three times a day: the rhythm of a modern phone game was invented for office computers.

## 2009 to 2010: Facebook finds a huge audience

Around 2009 games moved into social networks and found players who had never thought of themselves as gamers. Farming games taught tens of millions of people the habit of timers and return visits.

In 2010 a small studio called Casual Collective, later known as Kixeye, released **Backyard Monsters** on Facebook. You built a yard, ringed it with towers, bred monsters and sent them at other players' yards. Unlike the browser games before it, you could *watch* the attack: your creatures ran in and the defenses shot at them, with the defender nowhere near a keyboard.

That was the missing piece. Backyard Monsters is widely cited as a forerunner of what came next.

## 2012: Clash of Clans

In August 2012 the Finnish studio Supercell released **Clash of Clans** for the iPhone and iPad; the Android version followed in 2013. It took the build-and-raid loop and fitted it to a touchscreen:

- you **drop troops with a finger** and they fight by themselves;
- a raid takes **a few minutes**;
- results are **stars**: one, two or three;
- a **shield** protects you after you have been hit.

Clan wars, added in 2014, made it a team game. It became one of the most successful mobile games ever made, and it defined a genre so completely that "a game like Clash of Clans" is now simply how people describe the category.

Many followed: games with ships, with aeroplanes, with historical ages, with famous licences. Supercell's own **Boom Beach** (2014) was a notable variation.

## What changed along the way, and what did not

Follow the thread from 1992 and some things have barely moved:

- A **central building** whose level gates everything: the Construction Yard, the Town Hall, the Citadel.
- A **resource you must protect**, which is also the thing the enemy wants.
- A **rock-paper-scissors** of units and defenses.

Other things turned upside down:

| | 1992 | Today |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A session | An hour at a desk | Three minutes, anywhere |
| Your base | Gone when the match ends | Lives for months |
| Your opponent | Present, clicking | Usually absent |
| Control | Every unit, every second | Choose the army and the landing spot |
| Price | A box in a shop | Free, with optional purchases |

The modern form gives up fine control and gets something in return: a strategy game you can play well in the time it takes for a kettle to boil.

## Where Dune Clash fits

Dune Clash, which we released in November 2026, belongs to the last chapter: a persistent base, timers, three-minute raids, stars. It is an independent game, not connected with the Dune franchise or with Supercell; [the name is explained here](/blog/is-dune-clash-related-to-dune-or-clash-of-clans/).

It also borrows on purpose from the first chapter. The setting is a desert again. The army is infantry, vehicles and aircraft, as in the old real-time games, and guns can only hit some of them. And under the sand there is a worm that belongs to nobody.

A few choices are our own: [units that cost nothing and are always ready](/blog/dune-clash-units-guide/), two resources with separate jobs, and a server that [replays every raid to keep results honest](/blog/fair-play-server-replays-anti-cheat/).
