Put a desert in a story and sooner or later something enormous comes up through the sand. It has happened in novels, in films, in dozens of video games and, in a smaller way, on the real sea floor. This is a short tour of the idea, ending with the worm we built ourselves.

{{pic:worm|The Sandmaw of Dune Clash.}}

## Before the desert: worms were dragons

In old English a *wyrm* was not something you find in a garden. It was a serpent or a dragon. The north of England still tells the story of the **Lambton Worm**, a creature that grew until it could coil around a hill and had to be killed by a knight in spiked armour. Bram Stoker, the author of *Dracula*, wrote a late novel called *The Lair of the White Worm* (1911) about a monstrous serpent living under an English estate.

So when a modern writer needed a name for a vast legless thing under the ground, "worm" was waiting, with centuries of dread already attached.

## A legend from a real desert

The Gobi Desert has its own. Mongolian tradition speaks of the **olgoi-khorkhoi**, known in English as the Mongolian death worm: a thick red creature living under the sand, said to kill at a touch.

It reached Western readers through the American explorer Roy Chapman Andrews, whose 1926 book *On the Trail of Ancient Man* records Mongolian officials describing it to him. Andrews did not believe it existed, and no expedition since has found one. As a story, though, it has everything: the empty desert, the hidden animal, the danger of walking on the wrong patch of ground.

## 1965: Dune

Then came the book that fixed the image for good. In Frank Herbert's novel *Dune* (1965) the sandworms of the desert planet are hundreds of metres long, drawn to rhythmic vibration, and bound up with the most valuable substance in that universe. The people of the deep desert fear them, revere them and, famously, ride them.

Herbert's worms did what great inventions do: they made every later desert monster look like a reference to them. The novel has been filmed several times, most recently in the series directed by Denis Villeneuve, and each version has made the worm a little larger in the public imagination.

Dune Clash is not part of that universe and has nothing to do with its owners; [we explain the name here](/blog/is-dune-clash-related-to-dune-or-clash-of-clans/). But nobody builds a sand worm today without knowing who got there first.

## On screen

- ***Star Wars***. *The Empire Strikes Back* (1980) has a giant slug living in an asteroid; *Return of the Jedi* (1983) has the sarlacc, a mouth at the bottom of a pit in a desert called the Dune Sea.
- ***Beetlejuice*** (1988). Striped sandworms patrol a desert outside the house, played for comedy and still alarming.
- ***Tremors*** (1990). A small desert town is hunted by huge burrowing creatures that find their prey by vibration. The film turns one rule into ninety minutes of tension: do not touch the ground.

That rule is the heart of every good worm story. The monster is frightening, but the *ground* is the real threat, because you cannot stop standing on it.

## In games

Games took to the idea at once, because a worm is a designer's dream: it makes plain sand dangerous and needs very little drawing.

- **Dune II** (1992), the strategy game that shaped a genre, had sandworms that swallowed harvesters and tanks crossing open desert. They belonged to no side.
- ***The Legend of Zelda*** has had sand-burrowing bosses since the early 1990s, and *Breath of the Wild* (2017) has the Molduga, which swims under the dunes and lunges at the sound of footsteps.
- ***Final Fantasy*** has thrown sand worms at players in one desert after another.
- ***Diablo II*** (2000) fills its desert act with sand maggots.
- ***Mass Effect*** (2007) has thresher maws: you drive across an empty plain, and the plain stands up.
- ***World of Warcraft*** and ***Terraria*** both have giant worms as bosses.
- ***Dune: Awakening*** (2025), a licensed survival game, makes crossing open sand a gamble.

Different decades, different genres, the same lesson: empty ground is never empty.

## The real ones

Nothing the size of a train lives under any real desert. Big animals need a great deal of food, and sand offers little.

The sea floor is another matter. The **bobbit worm**, or sand striker, lives in warm seas, buried in the sediment with only its jaws showing. When a fish swims over, it strikes upward and drags the fish under. It can grow to about three metres.

It is an old way of life. In 2021 scientists described fossil burrows in rocks in north-eastern Taiwan, about twenty million years old, two metres long and L-shaped. They interpret them as the lairs of giant ambush worms much like the bobbit worm, the first fossil traces of such a hunter ever recognised.

So the scene of a worm bursting out of the sand under its prey is not pure invention. It has simply been made a thousand times bigger and moved out of the water.

## Why the idea keeps working

1. **You cannot see it.** The fear is in the waiting.
2. **There is a warning.** A tremor, a ripple, a sound. The audience, or the player, gets a moment to act.
3. **The rule is simple.** Stay off the sand, stay quiet, or stay in the air.
4. **It is nobody's ally.** A worm does not care who is winning.

A monster with a warning and a rule is not just scenery. It is a game mechanic waiting to happen.

## Our worm: the Sandmaw

When we made Dune Clash we wanted a desert that could bite, and we wanted it to be fair. So the Sandmaw follows the four points above exactly.

- **It is hidden**: it travels under the sand, under walls and buildings alike.
- **It gives a warning**: before every raid the scouting screen reads "Tremors: low" or "Tremors: high". It comes in {{n:wormChance}}% of raids.
- **Its rule is simple**: it hunts whatever walks or drives on the ground, bites everything within {{n:wormSplash}} cells and cannot reach aircraft.
- **It takes no side**: it ignores buildings, and the base's guns cannot see it.

Unlike most of its ancestors, it can be beaten. Above the sand it can be shot, and troops nearby turn on it together. It is tougher the bigger your army is, and it leaves after {{n:wormStay}} seconds either way.
